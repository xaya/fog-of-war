\begin{abstract}
Replicated deterministic state machines (blockchains and the state channels
built on them) make hidden information awkward: every replica must be able
to verify every transition, yet fog of war requires that positions stay
secret. Commit--reveal schemes hide positions but destroy \emph{encounters}:
two players walk past each other in the dark because neither may learn the
other is near. We present a \emph{blinded mutual-sighting test}: after both
players have hash-committed their moves for a round, and before either is
disclosed, the two clients run a Diffie--Hellman-blinded private membership
test (the Meadows~1986 / Huberman--Franklin--Hogg~1999 lineage, cut down from
set intersection to a single membership bit evaluated in both directions) over
Curve25519 with x-only Elligator~2 hashing. Both parties learn exactly one
shared bit (``we are in sight'') and nothing else. Two design choices
adapt the classical protocol to a consensus setting: every blinding scalar and
pad element is derived deterministically from a seed the player is already
bound to reveal, making the entire transcript recomputable by an after-the-fact
audit; and no conviction ever trusts the exchange itself, since cheating is
adjudicated by recomputing the sight predicate over tiles fixed by prior
commitments. Together these replace an in-round zero-knowledge proof, which
measurably does not fit our execution budget, with after-the-fact attribution.
The system is deployed inside a 78\,kB zero-import WebAssembly consensus blob;
an independent reimplementation reproduces its transcripts byte for byte. In
measured play the test eliminated unnoticed mutual-sight rounds entirely
(from 142 to 0) while an optimal Bayesian hunter absorbing every negative
answer still faced 211--925 candidate tiles and located a moving player in 0
of 64 matches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multiplayer games on public replicated state machines inherit a contradiction.
The chain (or channel) exists so that every participant can verify every
transition; fog of war exists so that participants \emph{cannot} see part of
the state. The standard resolution, commit now and reveal later, solves
only half of the problem. A hash commitment hides a player's position
perfectly, but perfect hiding is precisely what makes the game unplayable: if
neither player may learn anything about the other's position before the
endgame, then two players six tiles apart walk past each other in the dark,
every round, for the whole match. In our own dungeon-crawler testbed,
removing all position disclosure produced 142 rounds of mutual line of sight
in which neither player was told anything: an invisible non-game.

The requirement is therefore a conjunction:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Privacy.} Nobody learns anything about your position unless
they are close to you (or you spend it deliberately, e.g.\ on a contested
objective).
\item \textbf{Encounters.} Two players who \emph{are} close must both learn
so, in the same round, every round, with no exceptions.
\item \textbf{Adjudication.} Every rule above must remain enforceable by a
referee that never sees secret state, on a metered execution budget.
\end{enumerate}

The natural cryptographic answer to requirement~3 is a zero-knowledge proof
attached to every move. That answer is unavailable to us in-round: our
referee is a zero-import WebAssembly blob metered at 66.3~million units of
fuel per call, and a single Groth16 verification measures at 73.6~million.
This paper describes what we built instead.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{blinded mutual-sighting test} (\S\ref{sec:protocol}): a
two-party private membership test, run in both directions between move
commitment and move disclosure, that yields the one shared bit
``$\sight(a,b)$'' and nothing else. The construction is the classical DH
private-matching protocol of Meadows and of Huberman, Franklin and
Hogg~\cite{meadows86,hfh99}, specialised from set$\,\cap\,$set to
membership-of-one-element, instantiated x-only on Curve25519 with an
Elligator~2 hash-to-curve.
\item A \emph{determinism-for-auditability} discipline (\S\ref{sec:audit}):
every byte a party should send is a pure function of a seed it is already
committed to reveal, so a judge can recompute any disputed transcript after
the fact. Combined with signatures over flights, this converts ``prove your
input was honest'' (a SNARK's job) into ``lying is non-repudiable evidence,''
at negligible consensus cost.
\item \emph{Enforcement by geometry, not cryptography}
(\S\ref{sec:audit}): no conviction ever depends on the exchange. Suppressing
a sighting, or disputing a phantom one, are both adjudicated by recomputing
the public sight predicate over tiles bound by hash commitments made before
the round's information existed.
\item \emph{Measurements} (\S\ref{sec:impl}): the deployed system closes the
encounter hole completely (0 unnoticed mutual-sight rounds, previously 142)
at $\sim$100~ms of curve arithmetic per seat per round; an exact Bayesian
attacker fed every negative answer retains 211--925 candidate tiles and
located a moving player in 0 of 64 matches. An independent reimplementation
of every formula in this paper reproduces the deployed blob's transcripts
byte for byte.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setting and threat model}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Game channels.} The system runs on \emph{game channels}
\cite{kraft16} as deployed on the Xaya platform: two players play a
turn-based game off-chain, exchanging signed moves, with the blockchain as
the fallback arbiter. A single host process referees many different games by
loading each game's rules as a SHA-256-pinned, zero-import WebAssembly blob;
the blob is metered (66.3~M fuel per call) and its state capped (8\,192
bytes). Both clients and every potential arbiter run the identical blob, so
``the judge'' below means \emph{any} party replaying the rules; honesty of
the judge is by replication, not assumption.

\paragraph{Round structure.} Play proceeds in rounds on a fixed public map
(a $64\times 64$ tile dungeon, procedurally generated from a shared seed).
Each round:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Commit.} Both players publish hash commitments to their move
(tile and action, salted by a per-match 32-byte secret $\seed_i$).
\item \textbf{Sighting test.} The exchange of \S\ref{sec:protocol}, between
the two clients only; the referee is not involved.
\item \textbf{Disclose.} If the test said \emph{no}, a player publishes
nothing about its position. If it said \emph{yes}, both players are obliged
to publish their exact tiles for the round (with an arrival proof; on first
contact, the connecting path).
\end{enumerate}
Every match ends with an \textbf{endgame audit}: both players reveal
$\seed_i$ and their full paths, which the blob verifies against every
round's commitments and replays against the map. This audit pre-dates the
sighting test; \S\ref{sec:audit} extends it.

\paragraph{Adversary.} A malicious client controls all its own messages: it
may send arbitrary bytes in the sighting exchange, stall, or disclose
falsely. It cannot break hash commitments or signatures, and it cannot avoid
the endgame audit except by forfeiting (timeouts convert stalling into
forfeit). Both players' clients may be malicious simultaneously; the game
between two colluding cheaters is not our concern, but neither cheater may
gain against an honest opponent. The design rule throughout: \emph{consensus
never trusts a claim}; everything that convicts is recomputed by the blob
from committed data.

\section{The sight predicate}
\label{sec:predicate}

Positions are walkable tiles of the public map. For tiles $a,b$ let
$d(a,b)$ be the shortest walkable path distance (the implementation caps the
search at 9 steps) and let $\fov(a,b)$ be the game's line-of-sight predicate
(a symmetric, corner-aware visibility test on the tile grid). The predicate
jointly computed each round is
\begin{equation}
\sight(a,b) \;:=\; \bigl[\, d(a,b) \le 6 \,\bigr] \;\wedge\; \fov(a,b).
\label{eq:sight}
\end{equation}
$d$ is symmetric by construction; $\fov$ was verified symmetric exhaustively
over all 339\,757 in-range walkable pairs of the test maps. Symmetry makes
``the'' shared bit well defined: with
\begin{equation}
V(x) \;:=\; \{\, t \text{ walkable} : \sight(x,t) \,\},
\qquad
a \in V(b) \iff b \in V(a),
\label{eq:vset}
\end{equation}
and $x \in V(x)$ always (distance 0 satisfies \eqref{eq:sight}).
Over 1\,116 tiles sampled from 4 generated maps, $|V| \le 79$; the protocol
pads every set to a fixed $N = 128$ (\S\ref{sec:derive}), so message size and
work are constants independent of the sender's surroundings.

The predicate must be symmetric for the construction to apply at all:
asymmetric sight ranges would make the two parties' bits legitimately
disagree and the shared-bit framing collapse. Consequently the game defines
exactly one sight range in consensus; light sources are cosmetic.

\section{Cryptographic preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Group and encodings}

We work on Curve25519~\cite{bernstein06} in Montgomery form
$y^2 = x^3 + Ax^2 + x$ over $\Fp$, $p = 2^{255}-19$, $A = 486662$, with
prime-order subgroup of order
\begin{equation}
\ell \;=\; 2^{252} + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493
\end{equation}
and cofactor 8. All points are handled x-only (Montgomery $u$-coordinates)
as 32-byte canonical little-endian encodings, fully reduced mod $p$; point
equality is equality of encodings. Scalar multiplication is the
RFC~7748~\cite{rfc7748} Montgomery ladder run for a fixed 255 iterations,
\emph{without clamping}. Commutativity itself would survive clamping (each
clamped scalar is a fixed integer, and integer multiplication commutes); the
reason clamping is omitted is auditability: the transcript must be a pure
function of the derived scalar with no hidden transformation, so that a
judge recomputing a disputed element applies exactly the scalar of
\S\ref{sec:derive} and nothing else. Small-subgroup safety, clamping's usual
job, is provided instead by cofactor-clearing every point at creation
(\S\ref{sec:h2c}). The unclamped ladder core is validated against the
RFC~7748 \S5.2 known-answer vectors, including the 1\,000-fold iterated one,
through a clamping test harness.

Because encodings are x-only, $P$ and $-P$ are identified. A false positive
in the membership test would therefore require $P_a = \pm P_t$ for distinct
tiles; but hash-to-curve outputs \emph{are} x-coordinates, so the ``$-P$
collision'' and the ``x collision'' are the same event, absorbed into the
collision bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:correct}.

\subsection{Hash-to-curve}
\label{sec:h2c}

$\HtoC(m)$ is defined by:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $u \gets \sha(m)$ read little-endian, top bit cleared, reduced mod $p$.
\item x-only Elligator~2~\cite{elligator} with $Z = 2$ (a non-square in
$\Fp$ since $p \equiv 5 \pmod 8$):
\begin{equation}
x_1 = \frac{-A}{1 + Z u^2}, \qquad
g(x) = x^3 + A x^2 + x,
\end{equation}
with the exceptional case $Zu^2 = -1$ mapped to $x = -A$. If $g(x_1)$ is a
square in $\Fp$ (Euler's criterion, one exponentiation), output $x = x_1$;
otherwise output $x = -x_1 - A$ $\bigl(= x_1 Z u^2\bigr)$, for which
$g$ is then guaranteed square. No $y$-coordinate is ever computed; the
ladder needs only $x$.
\item Clear the cofactor by multiplying by 8, landing in the prime-order
subgroup.
\end{enumerate}

This is a \emph{nonuniform}, single-map, SHA-256-based encoding, not the
RFC~9380~\cite{rfc9380} suite
\texttt{curve25519\_\allowbreak XMD:\allowbreak SHA-512\_\allowbreak
ELL2\_\allowbreak RO\_}.
The choice is deliberate. The protocol needs (a) determinism, (b) points
with discrete logarithms unknown to everyone, and (c) outputs in the
prime-order subgroup. It does not need the map's output to be
indistinguishable from uniform, because every transmitted point is
additionally randomised by a per-round blinding scalar; and interoperability
is a non-goal, since every party runs the same pinned blob. The test suite
checks determinism, pairwise distinctness over sampled inputs, and the
identity $\ell \cdot P = \mathcal{O}$ for $\HtoC$ outputs, the last being
the strongest cheap statement, as it proves curve membership and cofactor
clearing in one identity (a twist point, or one with an 8-torsion component,
fails it).

\subsection{Derivations: nothing is random}
\label{sec:derive}

Every quantity in the exchange is derived, never sampled:
\begin{align}
\text{tile points (public):}\quad
& P_t = \HtoC\bigl(\mathtt{"DCHT"} \concat \mathrm{LE16}(\mathrm{morton}(t))\bigr),
\label{eq:tilepoint}\\[2pt]
\text{blinding scalar of player $i$, round $r$:}\quad
& \alpha_r^{(i)} = \sha\bigl(\mathtt{"DCHP"} \concat \seed_i \concat \mathrm{LE32}(r)\bigr),
\label{eq:alpha}\\[2pt]
\text{pad dummies of player $i$:}\quad
& D_j^{(i)} = \HtoC\bigl(\mathtt{"DCHD"} \concat \seed_i \concat \mathrm{LE16}(j)\bigr),
\quad j = |V|,\dots,N-1.
\label{eq:dummy}
\end{align}
The scalar of \eqref{eq:alpha} has its top 4 bits cleared, making it uniform
on $[0, 2^{252})$. Since
$\ell = 2^{252}(1+\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon \approx 3.8\cdot10^{-39}$,
the unreachable sliver of $[0,\ell)$ is negligible; and $\alpha$ is a
blinding factor, not a signature nonce, so nonce-bias attacks do not apply.
The measure-zero case $\alpha = 0$ is patched deterministically. The dummies
\eqref{eq:dummy} are intentionally round-independent: per-round freshness
comes entirely from $\alpha_r$, and fixed base points let a client
precompute every curve point it will ever hash (the maps are the expensive
half of the per-round cost).

$\seed_i$ is the same 32-byte secret that already salts player $i$'s move
commitments and is revealed in full at the endgame audit. That reuse is the
design (\S\ref{sec:audit}): it makes the honest transcript a pure function
of data the player is bound to expose.

\section{The protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Fix round $r$; both players have committed $(\text{tile},\text{action})$
for $r$. Write $\alpha_A = \alpha_r^{(A)}$, and let $a, b$ be the committed
tiles. Before any disclosure:

\paragraph{Flight 1 (both directions).}
\begin{equation}
A \to B:\quad
S_A = \mathrm{sort}\Bigl(
\bigl\{\, \alpha_A P_t : t \in V(a) \,\bigr\} \cup
\bigl\{\, \alpha_A D_j^{(A)} : j = |V(a)|,\dots,N-1 \,\bigr\}
\Bigr),
\qquad
Q_A = \alpha_A P_a,
\label{eq:flight1}
\end{equation}
and symmetrically $B \to A$: $(S_B, Q_B)$. Here $S$ is $N = 128$ points
(4\,096 bytes) plus $Q$ (32 bytes); $\mathrm{sort}$ orders by encoding
bytes, a canonical order independent of tile enumeration that reveals
nothing about which entries are dummies. Exactly $N{+}1$ hash-to-curve maps
and $N{+}1$ ladder multiplications are performed whatever $|V|$ is, so
neither length nor timing leaks the sender's surroundings.

\paragraph{Flight 2 (both directions).}
\begin{equation}
A \to B:\quad R_A = \alpha_A Q_B \;(= \alpha_A \alpha_B P_b),
\qquad\qquad
B \to A:\quad R_B = \alpha_B Q_A \;(= \alpha_B \alpha_A P_a).
\label{eq:flight2}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Local finish.}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{bit}_A = \bigl[\, R_B \in \{\alpha_A s : s \in S_B\} \,\bigr],
\qquad
\mathrm{bit}_B = \bigl[\, R_A \in \{\alpha_B s : s \in S_A\} \,\bigr],
\label{eq:finish}
\end{equation}
each costing $N$ ladder multiplications and $N$ encoding comparisons.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness]
\label{thm:correct}
Except with probability negligible in the hash length
($\ll 2^{-100}$ for a $4\,096$-tile universe under SHA-256),
$\mathrm{bit}_A = \mathrm{bit}_B = \sight(a,b)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
On the prime-order subgroup,
$\alpha_A(\alpha_B P_t) = (\alpha_A \alpha_B \bmod \ell) P_t
= \alpha_B(\alpha_A P_t)$, so $R_B = \alpha_A\alpha_B P_a$ equals
$\alpha_A(\alpha_B P_t)$ iff $P_a = P_t$ (x-only: iff $P_a = \pm P_t$,
the same hash-collision event, cf.\ \S\ref{sec:prelim}), i.e.\ iff
$a \in V(b)$, unless two distinct $\HtoC$ inputs collide, bounded by the
birthday term over the $\le 4\,096{+}128$ domain-separated inputs. Thus
$\mathrm{bit}_A = [a \in V(b)]$ and symmetrically
$\mathrm{bit}_B = [b \in V(a)]$; these coincide with $\sight(a,b)$ by
\eqref{eq:vset}.
\end{proof}

The deployed client asserts the two bits agree on every round it can observe
both, and treats disagreement as a defect, not an input.

\section{Security analysis}
\label{sec:security}

Designed leakage: the bit, and (by padding) nothing about $|V|$.

\paragraph{The blinded set hides its contents under DDH.}
Distinguishing $\{\alpha P_t : t \in T_0\}$ from
$\{\alpha P_t : t \in T_1\}$ for candidate tile sets $T_0, T_1$, given the
public $P_t$, is a standard DDH-type distinguishing problem in the
prime-order subgroup (x-only, exactly as in X25519). The receiver holds
nothing else involving $\alpha$.

\paragraph{Unknown discrete logarithms are load-bearing.}
The single most instructive pitfall we encountered:

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:hg}
If tile points were instead constructed as $P_t = h_t G$ with
$h_t = \sha(t)$ public, a receiver of one flight recovers the sender's
entire visible set, hence its position, in $O(|U|)$ scalar
multiplications, where $|U|$ is the tile universe.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take two blinded elements $X_1 = \alpha h_{t_1} G$ and
$X_2 = \alpha h_{t_2} G$. Every $h_v$ is public and invertible modulo $\ell$,
so form
\begin{equation}
S_i \;=\; \bigl\{\, h_v^{-1} X_i \;:\; v \in U \,\bigr\},
\qquad i = 1,2,
\end{equation}
at $|U|$ scalar multiplications each. Both sets contain $\alpha G$, attained at
$v = t_i$, and any further coincidence occurs only with negligible probability,
so $S_1 \cap S_2 = \{\alpha G\}$ is recovered by hash-table lookup with no
hypothesis testing. Given $\alpha G$, one further pass matching each element of
the flight against $\{h_v (\alpha G) : v \in U\}$ labels the whole set. The
total is $\approx 3|U|$ scalar multiplications, a few thousand for
$|U| \approx 4\,096$.
\end{proof}

With hash-to-curve, the analogous first step, obtaining $\alpha G$ from
$\alpha P_t$ without knowing $\log_G P_t$, is CDH. This is also why brute
force over the small tile universe buys nothing: ``is this element tile
$t$?'' has no checkable relation without breaking DH. The attack of
Proposition~\ref{prop:hg} is documented in the implementation's headers so
that no future ``simplification'' can silently reintroduce it.

\paragraph{Cross-round unlinkability.}
$\alpha_r$ is fresh per round, so linking $\alpha_r P_t$ to
$\alpha_{r+1} P_t$ (a stationary player) is again DDH. The dummies being
fixed per seed changes nothing: they are re-blinded like every tile.

\paragraph{Reflection and replay.}
$B$ may echo one of $A$'s own set elements back as its $Q_B$; then
$R_A = \alpha_A^2 P_t$. Exploiting the pair
$(\alpha_A P_t,\, \alpha_A^2 P_t)$ is the square-DH problem; no attack
without pairings is known, and Curve25519 is not pairing-friendly. The bit
$B$ computes from a garbage $Q$ is garbage (self-sabotage) and becomes
attributable evidence under \S\ref{sec:audit} once flights are signed.

\paragraph{Low-order and twist points.}
A malicious peer can substitute any 32-byte string for a point; the x-only
ladder processes off-curve (twist) and low-order inputs without
special-casing. The worst case is a small-order point sent as $Q$: the reply
$\alpha Q$ reveals $\alpha \bmod 8$ (the 8-torsion image survives because
the scalar is unclamped): at most 3 of $\alpha$'s 252 bits, useless
against DDH. The reply never matches a cofactor-cleared set element, so the
resulting bit is 0. Like every malformed flight, self-sabotage now and
signed evidence once attribution lands.

\paragraph{The accumulated-negative leak, quantified.}
Every ``no'' round tells a player that the opponent is outside its visible
set. These constraints accumulate; this is the designed privacy/liveness
trade of the whole scheme, and we measured it rather than assuming it away.
An exact Bayesian belief-set attacker (forward support propagation, the
optimal inference given the protocol's outputs, granted every advantage)
leaves a moving player \textbf{211--925 candidate tiles} of cover on our
maps; active probing strategies located the hider in \textbf{0 of 64
matches}. For comparison, the always-on coarse position beacon the test
replaced left $\sim$71 candidates. These figures must be re-checked if the
sight range or movement speeds change.

\paragraph{What the exchange deliberately does not provide.}
(i)~No in-round proof that $V$ was computed from the committed tile: a
malicious party can feed any set or $Q$ it likes; the surrounding system
makes this pointless or attributable rather than impossible
(\S\ref{sec:audit}). (ii)~No fairness or liveness: a party can stall
mid-exchange; the channel's existing timeout forfeits cover it. (iii)~It
requires the predicate to be symmetric (\S\ref{sec:predicate}).

\section{Attribution instead of zero-knowledge}
\label{sec:audit}

The referee never verifies the exchange in-round; the happy path costs
consensus nothing. Three facts combine into the enforcement story:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The honest transcript is a pure function.} By
\S\ref{sec:derive}, every byte party $i$ should send in round $r$ is
$T(\seed_i, r, \mathrm{tile}_i(r), \mathrm{map})$: the scalar from the seed,
$V$ from the committed tile and the public map, tile points and dummies from
fixed derivations, order fixed by the sort.
\item \textbf{The audit reveals the preimages.} The pre-existing endgame
audit forces both players to reveal $\seed_i$ and their full paths, verified
against every round's hash commitments. Any judge can therefore recompute,
after the fact, the exact flight bytes an honest party would have sent in
any round, and compare with what was sent. With flights ed25519-signed, a
wrong byte is non-repudiable evidence against its sender. The recomputation is
necessarily of a \emph{single named element}, not of the flight: we measured a
full flight rebuild at ${\approx}1.98\times10^9$ fuel, thirty times the entire
per-call cap, whereas verifying a supplied flight against its committed hash and
recomputing one element costs a measured 15.9~M, which sits alongside the
17.6~M endgame audit at 51\% of the 66.3~M cap that a single Groth16
verification already exceeds at 73.6~M. Binding the bytes therefore uses a
commitment the sender already publishes in its own signed move rather than a
signature scheme inside the blob, and that commitment is a Merkle root over the
flight rather than a flat hash: a dispute then carries one leaf and an eight-step
path, roughly 350 bytes, instead of the whole 4{,}128-byte flight.
\item \textbf{Convictions never trust the bit.} ``In sight and stayed
dark'' and ``disputed a sighting that was not there'' are both adjudicated
by recomputing $\sight$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:sight}) over tiles bound by
commitments made before the round's information existed. The bit only tells
honest clients when they are obliged to act; the map convicts.
\end{enumerate}

So the SNARK's job, \emph{prove your input was honest}, is replaced by:
\emph{your input is a deterministic function of things you are already bound
to reveal, and lying about it is signed evidence}. The guarantee is
delivered after the fact instead of in-round, which a turn-based channel
game with a mandatory endgame audit can afford; the consensus-side cost is
essentially zero.

\paragraph{The trust boundary, stated plainly.}
Until the signed-flight component is active, transcripts are deterministic
but not yet \emph{attributable}: a malicious opponent who feeds a fabricated
visible set can make an honest client's instrument read a phantom ``yes,''
causing it to over-disclose or to file a dispute it then loses. This framing
residual is pinned by a failing-by-design test in the deployed suite and is
closed exactly by item~2 above (signatures plus the one-element recompute
branch); it is the final component of the design, in progress at the time of
writing. Everything else in \S\ref{sec:security} holds already.

\section{Implementation and measurements}
\label{sec:impl}

The complete rules compile to a single zero-import WebAssembly blob of
78\,196 bytes: map generation, movement, combat, commitments, the audit,
and the entire sighting test (field arithmetic, ladder, Elligator~2,
derivations). The blob is executed identically by both clients (V8) and the
metered referee (wasmtime); there is no cryptographic library dependency,
and it is self-contained by construction.

\paragraph{Independent verification.}
Every formula in \S\S\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:protocol} was
reimplemented from this paper's text alone, in a separate codebase (BigInt
arithmetic, no shared code), and compared against the deployed blob:
full 4\,128-byte flight-1 messages from three positions, flight-2 responses,
and the finishing bit are reproduced \textbf{byte for byte} (20 checks, 0
failures), alongside the RFC~7748 known-answer vectors through the same
core. The prose and the deployed system cannot silently diverge without
that harness failing.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{The DH private-matching lineage.}
The double-blinding trick at the core of \S\ref{sec:protocol} is old and
good. Meadows~\cite{meadows86} used commutative blinding for cryptographic
matchmaking in 1986; Huberman, Franklin and Hogg~\cite{hfh99} used the same
DH structure for private community matching in 1999. The modern literature
knows it as ECDH-PSI: formalised and extended by Freedman, Nissim and
Pinkas~\cite{fnp04} and given linear-complexity DH instantiations by De
Cristofaro and Tsudik~\cite{dt10}. It runs at planetary scale today:
Google's Password Checkup~\cite{checkup19} and Apple's PSI
system~\cite{applepsi} are both descendants. Our protocol is this classical
construction \emph{reduced}: from set$\,\cap\,$set to
membership-of-one-element, evaluated in both directions, because the game
needs one shared bit, not the overlap.

\paragraph{The implementation we started from.}
Our engineering entry point into this literature was Thomson's open-source
ECDH-PSI implementation~\cite{thomsonpsi}, a clear reference implementation
of the classical protocol (hash-to-group, blind, exchange, double-blind,
with an encrypted-payload layer for revealing matched elements over NIST
P-256/secp256k1). Studying and prototyping against it shaped what we kept
and what we changed: we replaced the NIST curves with Curve25519 and an
explicit Elligator~2 hash-to-curve (Proposition~\ref{prop:hg} is what
happens otherwise for a small universe); dropped the payload-encryption
layer entirely (we must never learn \emph{which} element matched, so there
is nothing to decrypt); fixed the set cardinality by padding (otherwise
$|V|$ leaks for free); and, most importantly, bound the inputs. Like most
PSI reference implementations, \cite{thomsonpsi} leaves inputs unbound, so a
malicious party can use the protocol as a membership oracle over fabricated
sets; our committed-seed determinism and audit (\S\ref{sec:audit}) exist
precisely to close that gap. We have reported that observation upstream,
together with a concrete instance of Proposition~\ref{prop:hg} in that
codebase: its elements are mapped as $h_x G$ with $h_x$ public, and a
participant legitimately recovers the peer's blinding factor times the
generator during the exchange, from which it can derive the peer's payload key
for any candidate element and enumerate the peer's set offline. It is the
sharpest illustration we know of why hash-to-curve is not a stylistic detail.

\paragraph{Private equality and proximity testing.}
Comparing secrets without revealing them goes back at least to Fagin, Naor
and Winkler~\cite{fnw96}. The closest prior art to our setting is Narayanan
et al.'s private proximity testing~\cite{narayanan11}, which reduces
location proximity to private equality of grid cells for social
location-sharing. Our test differs in the predicate (walkable-path distance
plus line of sight on a dungeon map, not Euclidean grid overlap), in being
mandatory and bidirectional every round (their users choose when to query;
our players are \emph{obliged} to act on the bit, and the obligation is
enforced by consensus), and in the adversary handled (their protocols
assume honest-but-curious phones; our clients are fully malicious and
handled by attribution).

\paragraph{Hidden information in adversarial games.}
Mental Poker~\cite{sra79} founded the field of playing hidden-information
games without a trusted dealer. The modern blockchain line is exemplified by
Dark Forest~\cite{darkforest}, which achieves fog of war on a public chain
by attaching a zk-SNARK to every move, proving it valid relative to hidden
coordinates. That is the ``prove your input was honest'' path we could not
take: our referee's fuel cap (66.3~M) is measurably below one Groth16
verification (73.6~M), and channel games settle on chains where verifier
costs bind harder still. The determinism-plus-audit construction of
\S\ref{sec:audit} is our substitute, available to any turn-based game that
already ends in a full reveal. Note also the difference in what is proved:
Dark Forest proves unilateral facts about one player's hidden move, whereas
a sighting is an irreducibly \emph{joint} predicate over two players'
secrets, which is what forces a two-party protocol rather than a proof.

\paragraph{Infrastructure.}
Game channels are due to Kraft~\cite{kraft16}, deployed on Xaya, the
successor of Huntercoin (to our knowledge the first blockchain to run a
multiplayer game world fully on-chain), whose scaling and privacy limits
motivated this line of work. Curve25519 is Bernstein's~\cite{bernstein06};
the Elligator~2 map is from Bernstein, Hamburg, Krasnova and
Lange~\cite{elligator}; the ladder and its test vectors follow
RFC~7748~\cite{rfc7748}; RFC~9380~\cite{rfc9380} is the general
hash-to-curve framework from which \S\ref{sec:h2c} deliberately deviates.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Attribution is the last unbuilt component.} Signed flights
and the one-element recompute branch (\S\ref{sec:audit}) are designed and
test-pinned but in progress; until they land, fabricated flights can frame
an honest disputer, and a conservative client should weigh disputes
accordingly.
\item \textbf{A one-round rotation edge.} A suppressor whose disclosure
happens to be ordered after the honest player's can dodge a dispute for
that single round of fleeting edge-of-range contact; signed transcripts
close this fully.
\item \textbf{Two seats measured.} The exchange generalises pairwise (6
pairs at 4 seats, cost linear in pairs), but all measurements reported here
are two-seat.
\item \textbf{Cost.} $\sim$100~ms/seat/round of curve arithmetic is
acceptable at a 1.4~s beat and halves with precomputation; a worker thread
removes UI jank. Sub-100~ms beats would need batching or a faster field
implementation.
\item \textbf{The leak is a dial, not zero.} The accumulated-negative
constraint is inherent to guaranteed encounters: any protocol satisfying
requirement~2 of \S1 must leak at least the negatives. We chose to measure
and publish the resulting anonymity set (211--925 tiles) rather than claim
more than the construction gives.
\end{enumerate}
